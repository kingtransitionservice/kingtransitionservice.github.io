Car Accident Checklist: Exactly What to Document at the Scene

If you have just been in a crash, you are probably wondering what to document after a car accident — and in what order. Adrenaline makes details slippery, and the notes you take in the first hour matter more than anything you reconstruct later. This checklist walks you through it step by step, so nothing important slips away.

1. Make the scene safe first

Before you document anything, protect people. Turn on your hazard lights, and if the cars are drivable and it is safe to do so, move them out of active traffic to the shoulder. If anyone is hurt, call emergency services immediately and do not move injured people unless there is an immediate danger like fire. Your safety comes before your paperwork — every time.

2. Call the police and get a report number

Even for minor fender-benders, a police report creates an official, timestamped record. When officers arrive, give a calm, factual account: what happened, where, and when. Avoid guessing about speed or fault — stick to what you directly observed. Before they leave, ask for the report number and the officer’s name, badge number, and department. Write it down immediately; business cards get lost and memories fade.

3. Photograph everything: the 12-shot list

Your phone camera is the single most powerful documentation tool at a crash scene. Work through this shot list methodically:

  1. Wide shot of the whole scene from all four corners, showing both vehicles and their positions.
  2. Close-ups of every point of damage on all vehicles involved — yours and theirs.
  3. License plates of every vehicle, clearly readable.
  4. The other driver’s license and insurance card. Photograph both sides.
  5. Road conditions: skid marks, debris fields, potholes, wet or icy patches.
  6. Traffic signals and signs near the scene, including their state at the time.
  7. Street name signs or intersection markers to pin the exact location.
  8. Weather and lighting conditions — a quick shot of the sky tells the story.
  9. Your odometer and dashboard (airbag lights, warning indicators).
  10. Any visible injuries on yourself or passengers, even minor ones.
  11. Inside the other vehicle if visible — a phone mounted on the dash or an open laptop can matter.
  12. A short video walk-around of the full scene with you narrating the date, time, and location.
Tip: keep your phone’s location services on so every photo carries GPS metadata. That timestamped, geotagged evidence is hard to argue with.

4. Exchange information — all of it

Get the full details from every driver involved, not just the minimum:

  • Full name, phone number, and address
  • Driver’s license number and issuing state
  • Insurance company, policy number, and the claims phone number
  • Vehicle make, model, year, color, and VIN
  • Registered owner’s name if it differs from the driver

Offer the same information in return. Keep the exchange polite and factual — this is not the time to discuss who was at fault.

5. Talk to witnesses while they are still there

Witnesses leave. Phones come out, and then people drive away. If anyone stopped or watched, ask for their name and phone number and jot down — in their words — what they saw. Even two sentences from a neutral bystander can carry enormous weight later. If a nearby business might have security-camera footage, note the business name and address so footage can be requested before it is overwritten.

6. Write your own account the same day

Memory degrades fast. Within hours, sit down and write exactly what happened in your own words: where you were going, the lane you were in, what you saw, what you heard, what you felt on impact. Include the time, weather, and traffic conditions. Do not edit yourself or try to sound polished — raw and detailed beats smooth and vague. Date the note. This contemporaneous account is one of the most credible pieces of evidence you can own.

7. Build a post-accident timeline

The crash is only the beginning of the paper trail. Starting that evening, keep a running log:

  • Every medical visit, with provider names and what was treated
  • Symptoms as they appear — some injuries surface days later
  • Every call with insurance companies: date, representative name, what was said
  • Repair estimates and shop invoices
  • Missed work days and lost income
  • Any new photos as damage or injuries evolve

A scattered pile of papers and screenshots falls apart when you need it most. Keeping photos, notes, contacts, and a timeline in one organized place — like the RECORDYN app, which was built exactly for incident documentation — means you can pull up the full story in seconds instead of digging through your camera roll at the worst moment.

8. What NOT to do

  • Don’t admit fault at the scene — not to the other driver, not to witnesses, not on a recorded call. Fault is determined later, with evidence.
  • Don’t skip medical care because you “feel fine.” Adrenaline masks injuries; get checked the same day.
  • Don’t post about the crash on social media. Posts and photos can be used against your claim.
  • Don’t accept a quick settlement before you know the full extent of damage and injuries.
  • Don’t throw anything away — tow receipts, medical bracelets, damaged personal items are all evidence.

9. Dealing with insurance adjusters

Within days, you will likely hear from one or more insurance adjusters — yours, theirs, or both. Their job is to close your claim for as little as possible, and everything you say can be used to do that. Keep these rules in mind: give only the basic facts (date, location, vehicles involved), never speculate about injuries (“I’m fine” on day one means nothing if whiplash appears on day four), and do not give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer without understanding what you are agreeing to. Take notes on every conversation — date, name, what was discussed — and add them to your timeline the same day. If an adjuster pressures you to settle quickly, that is itself useful information: it usually means they know the claim is worth more than they are offering.

10. Medical documentation: the hidden half of your case

Vehicle damage is visible; injuries are not always. Soft-tissue injuries, concussions, and back problems often surface 24 to 72 hours after a crash, which is why same-day medical evaluation matters even when you feel okay. From that first visit onward, keep a dedicated medical file: ER discharge papers, imaging results, prescriptions, physical therapy referrals, and every follow-up appointment. Just as important, keep a simple daily symptom journal — pain levels, sleep disruption, activities you had to skip. A two-sentence entry each evening (“Neck stiff, couldn’t turn head fully; skipped gym”) becomes powerful evidence of how the accident affected your daily life, something bills alone can never show.

11. Hit-and-run or uninsured driver: extra steps

If the other driver flees or turns out to be uninsured, your documentation matters even more. Write down everything you remember immediately: partial plate, vehicle color and make, direction of travel, driver description. Ask witnesses specifically whether they caught the plate or have dashcam footage — nearby parked cars with dashcams are an overlooked goldmine. File the police report regardless, and notify your own insurer promptly, since uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage (if you carry it) becomes your path to recovery. Photograph the scene exactly as you would in a normal crash; the absence of the other driver does not reduce what you should capture.

12. Your 72-hour action checklist

To pull it all together, here is what the first three days should look like:

  • Day 0 (scene): safety, police, 12-photo shot list, information exchange, witness contacts, tow and location notes.
  • Day 1: write your full account, start the symptom journal, notify your insurer, get a medical evaluation, back up all photos to cloud storage.
  • Day 2: obtain the police report number, get repair estimates, log every call, photograph any developing bruises or vehicle damage you missed.
  • Day 3: review your timeline for gaps, follow up on medical referrals, and confirm your claim number in writing.

Do this, and you will walk into any insurance negotiation or attorney consultation with a file most people never manage to assemble.
